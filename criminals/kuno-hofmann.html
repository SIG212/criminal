Background

Kuno Hofmann was born in Nuremberg in 1951 into a family life marked by instability and violence. His father was an alcoholic, and his parents’ marriage dissolved when he was young. Hofmann himself proved unable to adapt to conventional societal structures. He abandoned a baker’s apprenticeship and later failed to complete training as a dental technician, exhibiting a profound lack of discipline and an inability to maintain employment. Socially isolated and with few friends, he developed an early and intense interest in the occult, particularly vampirism and death. His preoccupations led him to spend significant time in local cemeteries, where his macabre interests began to manifest in more than just idle curiosity. Before any homicides were committed, Hofmann engaged in the desecration of graves, exhuming recently interred corpses to perform ritualistic acts that included drinking their blood. These were not the actions of a calculated predator in waiting, but of a deeply disturbed individual whose violent fantasies were escalating toward direct human contact.

The Crimes

The transition from desecrating the dead to murdering the living occurred in late 1971. Hofmann’s violence appeared opportunistic, directed at individuals who were vulnerable or with whom he had a fleeting, often fraught, connection. His first known victim, on October 29, 1971, was Charles Knarr, a man with whom Hofmann had been drinking. The two argued, and Hofmann strangled him. His next victims were chosen for their perceived frailty. On January 23, 1972, he gained entry to the Nuremberg flat of Ruth Lissy, a 43-year-old housewife, strangling her and inflicting post-mortem stab wounds. Less than a month later, on February 12, he killed 76-year-old Klara Öhm in her home, again by strangulation. Investigators at the scenes found little in the way of forensic evidence to link the crimes, which seemed to lack a clear motive beyond the act of killing itself. The attacks were brutal but left behind no consistent signature, save for the vulnerability of the victims and the absence of theft or sexual assault in the conventional sense. His final act of violence was the attempted murder of his fiancée’s mother, Elfriede W., whom he strangled into unconsciousness but who ultimately survived.

Evasion

Hofmann’s ability to evade capture for several months was less a product of criminal cunning than of circumstance and the nature of his crimes. The victims were not connected to each other, making it difficult for investigators in the pre-digital era to establish a pattern. The murders of Lissy and Öhm were initially investigated as isolated incidents, with robbery being a suspected but unconfirmed motive. Hofmann was a social phantom; he had no steady job, no deep community ties, and moved between temporary lodgings, leaving a faint trail for authorities to follow. Furthermore, the sheer strangeness of his underlying compulsions was not immediately apparent to law enforcement. The reports of grave desecrations in Nuremberg's St. Johannis cemetery were treated as separate matters of vandalism, not as potential indicators of a developing homicide suspect. Hofmann operated without a clear plan, his actions driven by impulse, which rendered his movements unpredictable and his profile difficult to construct.

Capture

The investigation reached its turning point following the attack on Elfriede W. in February 1972. Her daughter, Hofmann’s fiancée, had grown increasingly fearful of his erratic behavior and bizarre confessions about communicating with the dead. After he attacked her mother, she went to the Nuremberg police and implicated him not only in the assault but also in the recent murders that had unsettled the city. Armed with this direct testimony, authorities located and arrested Kuno Hofmann at his latest place of work on February 19, 1972. In custody, he offered no resistance and provided a full confession to the murders, the attempted murder, and the extensive desecration of graves. His accounts were delivered in a detached, matter-of-fact tone, filled with graphic details of his necrophilic activities and his belief that drinking blood would grant him supernatural powers. During his trial, the court was presented with overwhelming psychiatric evidence detailing his severe personality disorders and necrosadistic impulses. Despite this, he was found legally sane, albeit with diminished responsibility, and sentenced to life in prison.

Legacy

The case of Kuno Hofmann left a lasting imprint on the German public consciousness, reigniting gothic folklore in a modern, industrial society. It presented the criminal justice and psychiatric systems of post-war West Germany with a subject who defied easy categorization. He was not a rational criminal mastermind, but a man whose profound mental illness manifested in acts of extreme violence and desecration. The media’s creation of the "Vampire of Nuremberg" moniker transformed a pathetic and dangerous individual into a figure of morbid fascination, blurring the line between clinical reality and supernatural horror. Ultimately, Hofmann’s story served as a stark case study in the failure of social and medical systems to identify and intervene in a mind disintegrating into violent psychosis. It revealed how a person could become functionally invisible to the institutions meant to provide support, emerging only when their private pathology erupted into public tragedy.
